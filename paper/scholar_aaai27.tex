\begin{abstract}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
Understanding and querying scientific papers demands precise, evidence-grounded
comprehension that general-purpose language models struggle to provide reliably.
Existing PDF-chat systems hallucinate citations, discard document structure, and
cannot trace generated claims back to verifiable source pages.
We present \textbf{ScholAR} (\textbf{S}mart \textbf{C}ompanion for \textbf{H}olistic \textbf{O}rganization, \textbf{L}iterature \textbf{A}nalysis \& \textbf{R}esearch), a retrieval-augmented generation framework designed
specifically for scientific document comprehension that runs \textbf{entirely on-device},
with no cloud API in the loop for either text or vision inference.
ScholAR enforces strict page-boundary-preserving text segmentation, ensuring that
every retrieved chunk maps exactly to a single PDF page.
An indirect citation grounding mechanism decouples LLM generation from
page-number prediction, replacing fragile in-context page references with
verifiable evidence identifiers that the frontend resolves to precise PDF highlights.
Beyond single-document text QA, ScholAR extends to \textbf{visual grounding}
(routing figure/table questions to a local, natively multimodal model) and
\textbf{multi-document citation-chasing} (resolving and ingesting a paper's
own references so questions can be answered against both the anchor paper and
its cited works).
We introduce a manually annotated retrieval benchmark spanning 14 query--chunk
pairs across 3 landmark NLP papers, evaluated using Recall@$K$ and
Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR).
Our BM25-primary retrieval system achieves Recall@5 of 0.929 and MRR of 0.788,
outperforming keyword-overlap baselines by 10.1 percentage points in MRR.
An NLI-based faithfulness metric (Combined Faithfulness Score, CFS) evaluated on
a 51-case oracle-claim benchmark shows hybrid BM25+Dense+RRF retrieval reaching
Combined CFS of 0.829 and 96.1\% of claims judged faithful.
On an 18-case visual-grounding pilot benchmark spanning four reasoning types,
retrieval correctly routes to the target figure or table in 18/18 cases.
On a 10-case multi-document locality benchmark, ScholAR retrieves the correct
cited paper within the top 5 results half of the time (Recall@5 0.50, MRR 0.183),
below a random-guessing floor computed over the same candidate pool (Recall@5
0.625) --- a clear open challenge, consistent with prior findings that even
large foundation models struggle at cross-paper localization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
Reading unfamiliar scientific papers is slow precisely where it matters
most: verifying that a claimed number or method detail says what a reader
(or an assistant) thinks it says. General-purpose LLM chat over an uploaded
PDF makes this worse before it helps --- it summarizes fluently but readily
invents page numbers, since nothing constrains a citation to text the model
actually saw. Existing scientific-RAG systems close part of this gap but
assume resources most readers lack locally: cloud backends, multi-million-paper
datastores, or both (Section~\ref{sec:related-sciRAG}). We instead ask what
grounded, citable QA looks like under a hard local-only constraint: a
single sub-15B model, no cloud API. ScholAR combines page-preserving
chunking (every chunk maps to one PDF page) with an indirect citation
mechanism (the model cites evidence IDs it actually retrieved, not
free-form page numbers), extended to figures/tables and a paper's own
cited references. Concretely, we contribute:
\begin{itemize}
  \item An end-to-end, \textbf{fully local} RAG framework for scientific document
        comprehension enforcing strict page-boundary-preserving text segmentation,
        with text and vision inference both on-device.
  \item An \textbf{indirect citation grounding} mechanism: the model cites only
        evidence IDs mapped to chunks it actually retrieved, preventing
        page-number hallucination by construction.
  \item Extensions to \textbf{visual question answering} over figures/tables and
        \textbf{multi-document citation-chasing}, resolving and ingesting a
        paper's own references for cross-paper questions.
  \item Quantitative benchmarks for all four capabilities
        (retrieval, faithfulness, visual grounding, multi-document locality),
        each with manually annotated cases and Recall@$K$/MRR-style metrics.
  \item Controlled \textbf{ablation experiments} comparing keyword overlap,
        BM25-only, and BM25-primary-with-reranking retrieval architectures, and
        comparing two local models (\texttt{qwen3.5:9b}, \texttt{gemma4:12b}) on
        visual grounding to characterize model choice for a fully on-device system.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}
% Cover: Lewis et al. (2020) RAG paper, ColBERT, BEIR benchmark,
% standard RAG pipelines and their PDF-specific limitations.
The standard RAG framework was introduced by \citet{lewis2020rag}.
BM25 probabilistic retrieval was formalised by \citet{robertson2009bm25}.
The BEIR benchmark \cite{thakur2021beir} provides a heterogeneous zero-shot evaluation suite for retrieval systems.

\subsection{Scientific Literature RAG Systems}
\label{sec:related-sciRAG}
PaperQA \cite{lala2023paperqa} was an early high-accuracy RAG agent for
scientific QA, relying on proprietary cloud LLMs throughout; its successor
PaperQA2~\cite{lala2024paperqa2} reports superhuman literature-synthesis
accuracy via an agentic, multi-backend (GPT-4-/Claude-class) design.
OpenScholar \cite{asai2024openscholar} substantially outperforms PaperQA2
and GPT-4o using an open retrieval pipeline, though generation still runs
through large hosted or self-hosted models. SciRAG \cite{ding2025scirag}
further improves over both via citation-graph-aware symbolic reasoning.
None targets a fully on-device deployment: all call a cloud API or assume
large self-hosted models. ScholAR instead targets consumer hardware,
running both text and vision through a single sub-15B-parameter local
model, trading some capability for zero data exposure and API dependency.

\subsection{Scientific Document Understanding}
% Cover: ScholarBERT, SciDuet, DOC2PPT, SlideTailor (AAAI-26),
% S2ORC, ACL Anthology, Grobid, Nougat for structured parsing.
GROBID \cite{lopez2009grobid} and Nougat \cite{blecher2023nougat} provide
structured parsing of academic PDFs into machine-readable formats.
SlideTailor \cite{zeng2026slidetailor} generates personalised slides from
papers but relies on the same raw PDF text extraction bottleneck ScholAR
addresses. The S2ORC corpus \cite{lo2020s2orc} provides a large-scale
resource of parsed academic papers.

\subsection{Visual Document Retrieval}
ColPali \cite{faysse2024colpali} and its successors index each document page
as a visual embedding, retrieving pages directly from image content without
OCR. ScholAR takes an architecturally different, cheaper bet: BM25 text
retrieval stays the primary signal for the common text-QA path, and only
figure/table-targeted queries route to a vision-capable model applied to a
cropped region around the identified figure.

\subsection{Multi-Modal and Multi-Document Scientific QA}
M3SciQA \cite{li2024m3sciqa} is the closest existing benchmark to ScholAR's
multi-document extension: it pairs each anchor paper with its cited
references, requiring multi-modal and cross-document reasoning, and finds
18 foundation models all substantially underperform humans at this task.
ScholAR's smaller multi-document locality benchmark
(Section~\ref{sec:multidoc-bench}) echoes this: cross-paper localization is
hard even when the correct reference is already in the retrieval pool.

\subsection{Question Answering over Documents}
% Cover: SQuAD, Natural Questions, TriviaQA, SciFact (fact verification),
% QuALITY (long document QA), QASPER (scientific paper QA).
QASPER \cite{dasigi2021qasper} is the closest existing benchmark to our task,
covering information-seeking QA over scientific papers.
SciFact \cite{wadden2020scifact} evaluates fact verification over biomedical literature.

\subsection{Citation Grounding and Faithfulness}
\label{sec:related-faithfulness}
\citet{gao2023attributing} study attribution in LLM generation;
their analysis shows that citation quality degrades sharply without retrieval grounding.
\citet{min2023factscore} propose FActScore, which decomposes generated text
into atomic claims and verifies each claim against a knowledge source, adopting
this decomposition strategy in our benchmark construction.
\citet{laban2022summac} introduce SummaC for consistency detection
in summarization, with two variants: SummaC-Conv (fine-tuned NLI) and SummaC-ZS
(zero-shot via sentence embeddings).
We implement SummaC-ZS because it does not require a fine-tuned NLI model
(e.g., DeBERTa-MNLI), making it reproducible without GPU infrastructure or API access.
\citet{zha2023alignscore} show that embedding-based alignment metrics
substantially outperform ROUGE-family metrics for hallucination detection;
this motivates our choice of SummaC-ZS over lexical alternatives.
RAGAS~\cite{es2024ragas} is the closest existing automated RAG-faithfulness
framework to our own CFS metric, but scores an answer against retrieved
context directly rather than decomposing it into atomic claims verified
against oracle evidence, which is the design choice we adopt from FActScore.

\section{Problem Formulation}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
Given a scientific PDF $D$, a user question $q$, and a corpus of extracted
chunks $\mathcal{C} = \{c_1, \ldots, c_n\}$ derived from $D$ (and, in
multi-document mode, from $D$'s cited references), the system must retrieve
a ranked subset $\mathcal{C}_q \subseteq \mathcal{C}$ and generate an answer
$\hat{a}$ grounded only in that evidence:

\begin{equation}
  \hat{a} = \underset{a}{\arg\max}\; P(a \mid q,\, \mathcal{C}_q)
  \quad \text{where} \quad
  \mathcal{C}_q = \text{Retrieve}(q, \mathcal{C}, k)
\end{equation}

Each chunk $c_i$ carries a source page number and, in multi-document mode, a
source paper identifier, so $\mathcal{C}_q$ always resolves back to a
specific page of a specific document. Generation attaches each claim in
$\hat{a}$ to an evidence identifier $e_j$ referencing a specific
$c_i \in \mathcal{C}_q$ (Section~\ref{sec:cfs-method}) rather than a
free-form page number, so citations are constrained to what was actually
retrieved regardless of $\text{Retrieve}$'s own precision. Visual grounding
(Section~\ref{sec:method-visual}) substitutes an image-conditioned
generation step when $q$ names a figure or table; multi-document mode
(Section~\ref{sec:method-multidoc}) enlarges $\mathcal{C}$ across papers
before $\text{Retrieve}$ is applied, without changing this formulation.

\section{Method}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Document Processing Pipeline}
Given a paper (an arXiv identifier or an uploaded PDF), ScholAR downloads or
accepts the file, extracts per-page text via PyMuPDF~\cite{pymupdf}, applies
page-preserving chunking (below) and figure/table extraction
(Section~\ref{sec:method-visual}), and persists the results as
\texttt{metadata.json}, \texttt{pages.json}, \texttt{chunks.json}, and
\texttt{figures.json}: raw PDF in, page-preserving chunks with source page
numbers and (in multi-document mode) source paper identifiers out, ready for
retrieval and citation-grounded generation.

\subsection{Page-Preserving Chunking}
Standard character- or token-window chunking routinely splits a chunk across
a page boundary, breaking the one-to-one mapping between a retrieved chunk
and the PDF page a citation should highlight. ScholAR instead chunks
strictly within each page: Algorithm~\ref{alg:chunking} slides a window of
$W{=}1400$ target words with $\delta{=}120$-word overlap over each page's
token stream independently, so a chunk never spans two pages. A lightweight
regex pass additionally tags each chunk with a detected section title
(Abstract, Method, Results, \ldots) when a heading-like line is found near
the top of the page; the retriever uses this as a section-hint signal below.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
  \caption{Page-Preserving Chunking}
  \label{alg:chunking}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Pages $P = \{p_1, \ldots, p_n\}$, target words $W$, overlap $\delta$
    \ENSURE Chunks $\mathcal{C}$
    \STATE $\mathcal{C} \leftarrow [\;]$
    \FOR{each page $p_i \in P$}
      \STATE $w \leftarrow \text{split}(p_i.\text{text})$
      \STATE $s \leftarrow 0$
      \WHILE{$s < |w|$}
        \STATE $e \leftarrow \min(s + W,\; |w|)$
        \STATE $\mathcal{C}.\text{append}(\text{Chunk}(w[s{:}e],\; p_i.\text{page}))$
        \STATE $s \leftarrow e - \delta$
      \ENDWHILE
    \ENDFOR
    \RETURN $\mathcal{C}$
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{BM25-Primary Retrieval with Heuristic Reranking}
\label{sec:method-retrieval}
The production retriever ranks chunks with Okapi BM25~\cite{robertson2009bm25}
($k_1{=}1.4$, $b{=}0.72$) as the primary signal, then applies small additive
heuristic boosts: a query-expansion dictionary maps research-specific terms
to related vocabulary (e.g., \emph{result} $\to$ \emph{accuracy, BLEU,
score}); an explicit page number adds a page-hint boost; query language
implying a paper section (e.g., \emph{limitation}, \emph{results}) adds a
section-hint boost on a matching chunk; and rhetorical phrase cues
(\emph{we propose}, \emph{we introduce}, \emph{outperforms}) add a small
boost, since claim-bearing sentences more often satisfy an
information-seeking query. Visual-cue boosting for figure/table chunks
(Section~\ref{sec:method-visual}) uses the same mechanism. We initially
explored a design where a dense/semantic score dominated ranking; our own
ablation (Table~\ref{tab:retrieval}) showed BM25-primary more reliable on
this benchmark, so semantic, section, and phrase signals stay light
rerankers, while dense retrieval is used only in the separate Hybrid
BM25+Dense+RRF configuration evaluated for faithfulness
(Section~\ref{sec:faith-bench}).

\subsection{Indirect Citation Grounding}
Free-form citation prompting --- asking the model to write, e.g., ``[p.\ 7]''
directly --- is fragile: the model has no reliable way to know which page a
claim came from and will readily hallucinate a plausible-looking one.
ScholAR instead assigns each piece of retrieved evidence a short-lived
evidence identifier (\texttt{E1}, \texttt{E2}, \ldots) for the current turn,
and the generation prompt instructs the model to cite \emph{only} these IDs,
never a page number directly. After generation, ScholAR discards any page
number the model wrote on its own and rewrites each valid evidence-ID
citation into a compact numbered reference (\texttt{[1]}, \texttt{[2]},
\ldots); the frontend resolves each reference back to its source chunk's
page and highlights the corresponding PDF region. The model only decides
\emph{which} evidence supports a claim; the mapping to actual page and
highlight is deterministic application logic, not a model output.

\subsection{Visual Grounding}
\label{sec:method-visual}
When a query is judged to reference visual content (via cue terms such as
``figure,'' ``table,'' or ``plot,'' or an explicit figure/table number),
retrieval boosts figure and table chunks so they can compete against longer
text chunks in the BM25 ranking. Each figure/table caption on a page is
detected via regex, and the region above it is rendered at $3\times$ zoom
and cached alongside the caption text. A query naming an explicit figure or
table number (e.g., ``explain figure 2'') is matched directly against that
figure's own label rather than relying on lexical overlap alone: our
tokenizer, like most BM25 implementations, only begins a token on a letter,
so ``Figure 2'' and ``Figure 14'' both reduce to \texttt{figure} once digits
are dropped, making them otherwise indistinguishable to the ranker. When the
top-ranked chunk is a figure or table, ScholAR base64-encodes the cached
image and sends it, with the caption and supporting text, to the same local
Ollama model used for text generation (both models are natively
multimodal) rather than a separate vision model or cloud API. If the image
is missing or the model call fails, the system degrades to a caption-only
extractive answer rather than failing outright.

\subsection{Multi-Document Extension}
\label{sec:method-multidoc}
ScholAR resolves an anchor paper's own bibliography and lets the user ingest
individual cited works into the same study session: for arXiv papers via the
Semantic Scholar API by arXiv ID; for uploaded PDFs, via an S2 title search
over the last several pages, regex-matched against numbered (\texttt{[N]})
and author--year (\texttt{Vaswani et al., 2017}) citation styles. Ingesting
a reference downloads its PDF, applies the same page-preserving chunking as
the anchor paper, and tags every chunk with a \texttt{source\_paper\_id}. At
query time, chunks from all loaded secondary papers merge with the anchor's
into one retrieval pool, and each citation is labeled with its source paper.

\subsection{Study Goal Generation (Product Feature, Not Independently Benchmarked)}
ScholAR generates eight paper-specific study goals per document using a local
Ollama model (\texttt{qwen3.5:9b} or \texttt{gemma4:12b}).
Each goal has a title, a 2--4 sentence description grounded in paper text,
and 3--5 recursive subquestions whose evidence chunks are resolved by
a second BM25 retrieval pass.
When no LLM is available, a fully extractive fallback generates goals
directly from sentence patterns in the abstract and introduction.
We describe this feature for completeness, but --- unlike retrieval,
faithfulness, visual grounding, and multi-document locality --- we do not
report a dedicated benchmark for it in this paper; it is not one of this
paper's evaluated contributions.

\subsection{Faithfulness Evaluation: SummaC-ZS + Semantic Coverage}
\label{sec:cfs-method}
We evaluate citation faithfulness using the SummaC-ZS
protocol~\cite{laban2022summac}, the zero-shot variant of SummaC that does
not require a fine-tuned NLI model (Section~\ref{sec:related-faithfulness}
motivates this choice over ROUGE-family metrics).

\textbf{Tier 1: SummaC-ZS (Semantic Entailment).}
Each ground-truth claim $c$ is first decomposed into atomic sub-claims
$\{a_1,\ldots,a_m\}$ via sentence- and clause-level splitting
(FActScore-style~\cite{min2023factscore}).
For each atom $a_i$ and each retrieved chunk $\mathcal{C}_j$, we compute:
\begin{equation}
  s(a_i, \mathcal{C}_j) = \max_{t \in \text{sents}(\mathcal{C}_j)} \cos\!\bigl(\phi(a_i),\, \phi(t)\bigr)
  \label{eq:summac-zs}
\end{equation}
where $\phi(\cdot)$ is the \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} sentence encoder~\cite{reimers2019sbert}
loaded from the local HuggingFace cache (no network access required),
and the $\max$ is over all sentences in the chunk.
Atom $a_i$ is classified \textsc{Entailment} if
$\max_j s(a_i, \mathcal{C}_j) \geq \tau_e$ (threshold $\tau_e = 0.42$),
\textsc{Neutral} if $\geq \tau_n = 0.25$, and \textsc{Contradiction} otherwise.
Numeric claims containing values not present in any retrieved chunk are
down-graded from \textsc{Entailment} to \textsc{Neutral}.
$\tau_e$, $\tau_n$, and the Faithful cutoff (CFS $\geq 0.55$, below) were
calibrated manually against a small pilot set of claims with obvious
gold labels; we have not run a systematic sensitivity analysis over these
thresholds, which we flag as necessary follow-up rather than treat as settled.
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SummaC\text{-}ZS}(c, \mathcal{C}) =
  \frac{|\{a_i : \mathrm{label}(a_i) = E\}|}{m}
  \label{eq:nli-cfs}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Tier 2: SCR (Semantic Coverage Rate).}
Whole-claim vs.\ best-chunk cosine similarity (a coarser, complementary signal):
$\mathrm{SCR}(c, \mathcal{C}) = \max_j \cos(\phi(c), \phi(\mathcal{C}_j))$.

\textbf{Tier 3: KFP (Key Fact Presence).}
Fraction of numeric values and technical proper nouns from the claim
found verbatim in the top-5 retrieved chunks (exact lexical recall for
quantities and named entities, where semantics cannot substitute).

The three tiers combine into a single Combined Faithfulness Score (CFS):
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{CFS} = 0.50 \times \mathrm{SummaC\text{-}ZS} + 0.30 \times \mathrm{SCR} + 0.20 \times \mathrm{KFP}
  \label{eq:cfs}
\end{equation}

\subsection{System Architecture}
ScholAR is built as a fully local system.
The backend is a FastAPI application that stores parsed PDFs as JSON files
on disk (\texttt{metadata.json}, \texttt{pages.json}, \texttt{chunks.json}).
The frontend is a Next.js single-page application with an embedded
pdf.js viewer that highlights cited pages in real time.
All inference, both text generation and figure/table visual question
answering, is performed locally via Ollama using a single natively
multimodal model (\texttt{qwen3.5:9b} or \texttt{gemma4:12b}); no cloud
API is used anywhere in the pipeline.
No user data ever leaves the local machine.
All experiments in this paper were run on a consumer laptop (Apple Silicon,
18\,GB unified memory) under Python 3.12, PyTorch, and Ollama, with
\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}~\cite{reimers2019sbert} loaded from a local
Hugging Face cache for the faithfulness pipeline; no discrete or cloud GPU
was used for any reported result --- inference runs via Ollama's
Metal-accelerated backend on the laptop's own integrated GPU.

\section{Evaluation}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Retrieval Benchmark}
We evaluate retrieval quality on a manually annotated benchmark of 14 cases
spanning three landmark NLP papers:
\textit{Attention Is All You Need} (1706.03762~\cite{vaswani2017attention}),
\textit{Retrieval-Augmented Generation} (2005.11401~\cite{lewis2020rag}),
and \textit{LLaMA} (2302.13971~\cite{touvron2023llama}).
Each case specifies a user-style question, a set of expected relevant chunk IDs,
and the expected source pages.
Query types include main idea, architecture, training details, result tables,
human evaluation, safety analysis, and page-hint queries.
We report Recall@$K$ ($K \in \{1, 3, 5\}$) and Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR).

\subsection{Faithfulness Benchmark}
\label{sec:faith-bench}
For the faithfulness evaluation, we construct \textbf{51 oracle-claim cases} manually
annotated across three landmark NLP papers:
\textit{Attention Is All You Need}~\cite{vaswani2017attention} (15 cases),
\textit{RAG}~\cite{lewis2020rag} (12 cases), and
\textit{LLaMA}~\cite{touvron2023llama} (24 cases).
Each case contains a natural-language query, a ground-truth claim,
a manually verified supporting chunk ID, and a claim type label.
Claim types include: \texttt{result\_number} (13), \texttt{training\_detail} (8),
\texttt{technical\_claim} (11), \texttt{architecture\_detail} (10),
\texttt{formula} (1), \texttt{conceptual\_claim} (5),
\texttt{human\_eval} (2), and \texttt{environmental\_claim} (1).
All annotations were produced manually by the authors.

\subsection{Visual Grounding Benchmark}
\label{sec:visual-bench}
We construct an 18-case pilot benchmark spanning four reasoning types:
\texttt{visual\_understanding} (6), \texttt{data\_extraction} (5),
\texttt{comparison} (4), and \texttt{location} (3), each specifying a
figure/table-targeted question and the expected label(s). We report two
signals: \emph{retrieval grounding} --- whether the correct figure or table
ranks first, isolating retrieval (Section~\ref{sec:method-visual}) from the
vision model's reading accuracy --- and a lightweight \emph{answer-quality}
proxy (fraction of non-trivial question content words that reappear in the
answer). Under \texttt{gemma4:12b}, retrieval grounding is 18/18 with mean
answer score 0.731 and no caption-only fallback. Table~\ref{tab:visual}
reports retrieval by reasoning type. We deliberately keep this benchmark
small and frame it as a pilot: it validates the routing mechanism and gives
a first read on answer quality, not that visual QA is solved.

M3SciQA~\cite{li2024m3sciqa}: 15 \emph{locality} cases requiring the system
to identify which cited reference (not the anchor paper) contains the
answer, and 3 \emph{detail} cases answerable from the anchor alone as a
sanity check. Of the locality cases, 10 reference arXiv-resolvable works
(\texttt{locality\_arxiv}, our primary metric); the remaining 5 reference
pre-arXiv works or datasets (e.g., WMT, Dropout) that cannot currently be
downloaded and always score zero.

    Detail (anchor)    & 3  & \textbf{1.000} & \textbf{1.000} & \textbf{1.000} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The correct cited paper is recoverable within the top 5 results only half
the time (50\%) and essentially never ranks first (0\%): with dozens of
papers' chunks merged into a single flat retrieval pool, a niche secondary
paper must win on lexical match alone against the anchor paper's typically
larger, more topically central chunk set. Section~\ref{sec:discussion-multidoc}
discusses this finding relative to M3SciQA's comparable observation that even
large foundation models struggle at this exact task.

\textbf{A chunk-ID collision bug inflated an earlier version of this
result.} We initially measured Locality (arXiv) at R@5 0.800 / MRR 0.356.
Per-paper chunk numbering restarts at \texttt{chunk\_001} for every paper,
and the retrieval scorer keyed its BM25 score cache by this
non-globally-unique ID rather than list position, so merged multi-document
chunks with colliding IDs silently inherited each other's scores (verified:
15 of 34 chunks collided when merging two representative papers). The
numbers above and in Table~\ref{tab:multidoc-bounds} are corrected after the
fix, reported the same way we reported the tokenizer-fix regression in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion-multidoc}: as a genuine finding, not a number
to quietly revise.

To give this number honest context rather than leaving it unexplained, we
compute two reference bounds on the 10 \texttt{locality\_arxiv} cases
(average 8.0 candidate secondary papers per case): a \emph{random floor} ---
the expected recall of picking a candidate paper uniformly at random with no
retrieval signal at all --- and an \emph{oracle} upper bound, where retrieval
is restricted to only the anchor paper plus the single correct secondary
paper (i.e., the paper-selection problem is assumed solved, isolating
chunk-level retrieval precision \emph{within} the correct paper).
Table~\ref{tab:multidoc-bounds} reports all three.

discusses what this revises about our earlier interpretation.

\subsection{Does Reading the Image Help? A Caption-Only Ablation}
\label{sec:caption-ablation}
Rather than implement a full ColPali-style~\cite{faysse2024colpali} visual
embedding baseline, we run a cheaper, more direct ablation: send the
identical prompt to the same local model \emph{without} the figure image
attached, scored with the same heuristic. Under \texttt{gemma4:12b},
caption-only scores \textbf{0.752} versus \textbf{0.731} for full vision ---
removing the image does not lower this particular score.

We do not read this as evidence that visual reading is unnecessary. Many
questions ask about a value also restated in the paper's prose near the
figure, so a text-only answer can be correct without the image; and our
proxy rewards lexical overlap with the \emph{question} rather than grounded
correctness, so a model reporting fine-grained visual details absent from
the question wording is not credited for them. Separating these effects
properly needs benchmark cases whose answer is genuinely absent from the
prose plus a correctness-based judge, not a lexical-overlap proxy --- we
flag this as follow-up work rather than overstate what this ablation shows.

\subsection{Baseline Systems}
For retrieval, we compare five configurations:
(1) \textbf{Keyword Overlap}: Jaccard-style term overlap with page-hint boost;
(2) \textbf{BM25-Only}: standard BM25 with page-hint boost;
(3) \textbf{BM25+Rerank (No PH)}: BM25 without page hints;
(4) \textbf{BM25+Rerank (PH)}: full system with page hints (default);
(5) \textbf{Dense-Only}: single-pass \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} cosine similarity,
no BM25, no rerank, no page hints --- the naive-RAG point in the
lexical-to-dense taxonomy~\cite{thakur2021beir}.
For faithfulness, we compare:
(6) \textbf{BM25-primary}: the ScholAR production retriever;
(7) \textbf{Hybrid BM25+Dense+RRF}: dense retrieval fused via Reciprocal Rank Fusion~\cite{cormack2009rrf}.

\subsection{Retrieval Results}
Table~\ref{tab:retrieval} shows the retrieval results.
BM25-primary outperforms keyword overlap on R@1, R@3, and MRR, and ties it on
R@5.
Page hints do not change the aggregate MRR; the two page-hint-specific
cases individually retrieve byte-identical rankings with and without hints,
so we cannot claim a measurable benefit even per-case here. More broadly,
none of Section~\ref{sec:method-retrieval}'s reranking heuristics move
BM25-Only, BM25+Rerank~(No~PH), and BM25+Rerank~(PH) apart on any metric ---

\section{Discussion}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

\textbf{Hybrid retrieval improves semantic recall.}
BM25-primary achieves Recall@5 of 0.929 and MRR of 0.788, confirming lexical
matching over page-preserving chunks as a reliable foundation. Hybrid dense
retrieval adds meaningful value on top: SCHR@5 improves from 0.824 to 0.922
(+9.8 pp) and Combined CFS rises from 0.809 to 0.829 (+2.0 pp), driven by
the dense component surfacing relevant chunks when exact BM25 terms are
absent.

\textbf{SummaC-ZS detects paraphrased entailment that ROUGE cannot.}
Our Tier~1 SummaC-ZS score is 0.941 for BM25-primary and 0.961 for Hybrid ---
higher than a purely lexical entailment check would likely yield on the same
cases, for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:related-faithfulness}
(sentence-level embedding similarity captures paraphrase, ROUGE does not).
The SCR tier (whole-claim cosine, 0.484 BM25 vs.\ 0.509 Hybrid) is the primary
differentiating signal between the two retrieval systems.

\textbf{Training-detail failures and retrieval depth.} The two non-FAITHFUL
cases (Section~\ref{sec:faith-bench}) both involve numerical evidence spread
across non-contiguous pages, validating structured extraction as
highest-priority future work; the monotonic Top-1-to-Top-5 CFS gains
(Table~\ref{tab:rank-ablation}) confirm the $K{=}5$ default is well-motivated.

\textbf{Tokenizer fixes trade off single-document precision against
compound-term matching; hybrid retrieval absorbs the cost better than
BM25 alone.}
We fixed a tokenizer bug uncovered during multi-document testing: token
boundaries only start on a letter, so a fused compound proper noun (e.g.\ a
figure or paper name written as one word) and its space-separated form
reduce to different token sets, breaking exact-name retrieval. The fix is
necessary for Sections~\ref{sec:method-visual}
and~\ref{sec:method-multidoc}, but perturbs BM25's length normalisation
globally: it cost BM25-only Recall@5 seven points (1.00 $\to$ 0.929) and
introduced one new faithfulness miss (\texttt{rag\_trivia\_score}). Hybrid
BM25+Dense+RRF was materially more robust --- barely moved, and still
recovers the case BM25 now misses.

\textbf{The multi-document pipeline is weak, and not only for the reason we
first thought.} \label{sec:discussion-multidoc}
The multi-document locality benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:multidoc-bench})
shows Recall@5 of 0.50 and Recall@1 of 0.00, both at or below the
random-guessing floor over the same pool (Table~\ref{tab:multidoc-bounds}).
We had attributed this entirely to flat retrieval --- a niche cited paper
competing against the anchor paper's larger, more central chunk set in one
shared BM25 pool --- and a strong oracle bound seemed to confirm that
isolating the correct paper would mostly fix it. That oracle number was
inflated by the chunk-ID collision bug (Section~\ref{sec:multidoc-bench}):
corrected, it shows chunk-level retrieval failing to rank the right chunk
first even with paper-selection solved (R@1 0.00, MRR 0.289). So this is two
compounding weaknesses, not one: cross-paper competition \emph{and} a
within-paper retrieval difficulty on these specific cross-document
questions --- mirroring M3SciQA~\cite{li2024m3sciqa}'s finding that even
large foundation models underperform humans here, though our corrected
numbers suggest the difficulty runs deeper than paper selection alone. We
still view per-paper-aware retrieval/reranking and the Recursive Language
Model pattern~\cite{zhang2025rlm} as promising, but no longer sufficient on
their own without diagnosing why oracle-setting retrieval underperforms.

\textbf{Baseline scope.} Section~\ref{sec:related-sciRAG} explains why
OpenScholar, PaperQA2~\cite{lala2024paperqa2}, and SciRAG are discussed
qualitatively, not head-to-head: none targets a consumer laptop, so a fair
comparison needs a resource-matched setup, left to future work.


% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Future Work}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

The training-detail failure mode (Section~\ref{sec:faith-bench}) motivates
structured extraction next: integrating Nougat~\cite{blecher2023nougat} for
LaTeX-preserving extraction, evaluated against
ColPali~\cite{faysse2024colpali}-style visual retrieval as an alternative
to our caption-and-crop grounding. We also plan to evaluate the Recursive
Language Model pattern~\cite{zhang2025rlm} for multi-document
citation-chasing, our clearest open problem.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

ScholAR grounds scientific QA in page-preserving chunks and indirect
citation IDs, extended to visual and multi-document QA. Retrieval,
faithfulness, and visual grounding are strong; multi-document locality
remains open.
