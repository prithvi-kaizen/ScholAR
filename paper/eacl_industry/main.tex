\begin{abstract}
Private and unpublished scientific PDFs motivate document assistants that can run locally and expose the source behind an answer. We present \textsc{scholar}, a local-first assistant with source-scoped evidence identities, an auditable answer trace, and bounded post-generation repair. We evaluate practical design choices on 100 retrospective retrieval questions and 50 paired generation cases on an 18\,GB workstation. BM25 retrieves a gold page first for 84\% of questions; adding dense retrieval reaches 85\%, whereas a modality-ranking heuristic reduces first-page hits to 59\%. Repair raises automatic reference-unit overlap F1 from 0.159 to 0.224 but lowers cited-page alignment from 0.670 to 0.618 on the 20 answerable cases with page labels. In 20 cross-paper controls, the pipeline's abstained status falls from 13 to 5 cases after repair. A separate LLM-based evaluation of 150 legacy answers records 59 correct, 33 partly correct, 20 incorrect, and 38 not judgeable. These are retrospective model judgments, not human ratings. They show that inspectable provenance does not establish support, and that retrieval, repair, and refusal policies need separate validation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Conclusion}
\textsc{scholar} makes local document assistance inspectable by preserving evidence identity and separating raw generation, citation mapping, lexical checks, repair, and terminal status. The retrospective study reveals a near-null dense-retrieval gain, a harmful modality-ranking heuristic, and a repair policy that raises lexical overlap while worsening cited-page alignment and abstained-status frequency. Among 113 reference-eligible cases in the LLM-based evaluation, 59 are labeled correct, 33 partly correct, 20 incorrect, and one not judgeable; the retained evaluator identity is insufficient for a named-model claim. These findings argue for independent evidence and refusal judgments before treating provenance or repair as reliability guarantees. The prepared human study has not yet received ratings, and a paper-disjoint human evaluation and real-user deployment study remain necessary.
